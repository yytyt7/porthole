## ✨ What porthole Actually Does

Two things, both visual.

The first is the tunnel switch. An SSH port forward is a private doorway between your computer and another computer somewhere else. When the doorway is open, traffic can pass through it. When it is closed, nothing passes. porthole gives you a switch for each doorway, so opening and closing them becomes a click instead of a command.

The second is the port list. Every program that accepts connections on your computer must "listen" on a port first. Think of a port like a numbered door in a large building. porthole shows you all the doors that are currently open and waiting. This is useful when you want to know why something is not connecting, or when you simply want to see what is running in the background.

## 🔀 Turning Tunnels On and Off

Open the porthole panel from the system tray. You will see your tunnel entries listed. Each one has a control you can flip. When you flip it on, porthole starts the connection in the background and marks the entry as active. When you flip it off, the connection is closed cleanly and the entry returns to its resting state.

Because the switch lives in the tray, you never have to keep a terminal window open just to hold a tunnel. Close your other windows, get on with your work, and the tunnel stays where you left it. When you are finished for the day, one click closes it again.

The activity indicator matters here. Before you click, look at the state shown next to the entry. A tunnel that is on should look clearly different from one that is off. That single visual cue prevents the most common mistake: starting a tunnel that was already running, or closing one you still needed.

## 👀 See Every Port Listening on Your Machine

The port view is the quiet hero of porthole. It lists the ports that are open and listening, with enough detail to tell them apart. You can see which port number is in use, what is holding it, and whether it is local or exposed.

Why should a normal computer user care? Three everyday reasons:

- Something will not start because "the port is already in use." Now you can see what took it.
- You want to check that a service you just turned on is actually up.
- You are curious about what your machine is doing while you are not looking.

No command line, no memorized flags, no reading through pages of output. Just a list, in plain view, refreshed as things change.

## 🖥️ Living in the System Tray

porthole is designed to be ignored until you need it. The tray icon stays out of the way. Click once and the panel appears. Click away and it closes. It does not pop up notifications you did not ask for, and it does not take over your screen.

This is the right shape for a tool like this. Tunnel work happens in bursts. You need it for ten minutes, then you do not need it for three days. A tray widget matches that rhythm better than a full window ever could.

## 🔒 Safety and Privacy

porthole shows you what is happening on your own machine. It does not send your port list anywhere, and it does not need a cloud account. The information you see in the panel is the same information your computer already knows about itself, simply presented in a friendlier form.

When you close a tunnel, it actually closes. An entry that looks off is off. That predictability is the whole point of a switch.

## ❓ Common Questions

Do I need to know commands? No. The switch replaces the command for everyday use.

Will it show ports I did not open myself? Yes. Anything listening on your machine appears in the list, whether you started it or the system did.

Can I have several tunnels at once? Yes. Each entry has its own state, so you can mix active and inactive freely.

What does the indicator mean? It reflects the current state of that entry right now. Trust it over your memory.

Does it work outside the tray? The tray is the home. That is where it is meant to be used.
